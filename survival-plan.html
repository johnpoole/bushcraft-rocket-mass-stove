A season on Great Slave Lake

A plan for one person to last from September into winter on the east arm of Great Slave Lake, in the Northwest Territories, living on fish, snared hares and foraged food, in the shelter between three trees with the rocket mass stove.

The place

  • Thin boreal forest of spruce, jack pine and birch on bare granite, about 400 km south of the Arctic Circle.
  • Days shorten fast from September. Frost comes in September, snow stays from October, and winter nights reach −30 °C and colder.
  • The lake is deep, clear and cold, with lake trout, whitefish, northern pike and burbot. Arctic grayling live in the streams that run into it.
  • Snowshoe hares, grouse, red squirrels, black bears, wolves, foxes and wolverines share the shore.
  • Small sheltered bays freeze first, often in November. The open lake freezes later. Between open water and safe ice there are weeks when you can fish neither way.

The rules this plan follows

  • Fishing and snares are allowed. No hunting with a weapon.
  • Ten items. This plan takes: sleeping bag, axe, saw, knife, cooking pot, ferro rod, tarp, gill net, fishing line and hooks, snare wire. If the rules for your season do not allow a gill net, take a second spool of line and more hooks, and expect far less fish.

How much food it takes

Working outdoors in the cold, cutting wood and hauling water, you burn about 3,000 to 4,000 calories a day. Plan on 3,500. The figures below are rough averages, per 100 g of the part you eat.

A fish is about 60% flesh by weight. So 1 kg of whole lake trout gives about 900 calories, and 1 kg of whole pike about 550. A full day on fish alone takes about 4 kg of whole lake trout or whitefish, or 6 kg of pike.

Eat the fat. Hares and pike are lean. Living mostly on lean meat starves you even with a full stomach, because the body cannot run on protein alone. Fat from trout, whitefish and burbot liver has to come with them. Eat fish skin, eyes, livers and roe, and boil the heads and bones into broth.

Your own fat

The shortfall comes out of your body. Each kilogram of body fat holds about 7,700 calories. Gain weight before you go. Arriving with 10 kg more fat than usual carries you through about five weeks of half rations.

The season

Mid-September to October: open water

This is the month that decides the season. Catch and store more food than you eat.

  • First three days: choose the site and set the gill net, then put up the shelter frame and the tarp roof. Sleep under the tarp.
  • Every day: check the net at dawn and dusk. Set baited lines off rocky points for lake trout and in the weedy bays for pike.
  • Store fish: split them, hang them on a rack over a smoky fire of green wood, and turn them until they are dry and hard. Aim for 40 to 60 kg of whole fish stored before freeze-up.
  • Pick: every berry and rose hip you can reach while you walk to the net. Dry some on bark in the sun and by the fire.
  • Snares: set 15 to 20 along hare trails in the young spruce and willow, low and in narrow gaps. Check them every morning.
  • Build: walls, then stage one of the stove in the first two weeks.

November to December: freeze-up

The lake grows ice too thin to walk on and too thick to fish through open water. Live on the store and your own fat.

  • Eat the smoked fish at about half rations. Keep the snare line going.
  • Split dead wood every day for the stove, and eat the grubs and ants you find in it.
  • Build stage two of the stove, the bench, if the weather is dry.
  • Once a small bay holds at least 10 cm of clear ice, start fishing through it. Test with the axe every few steps as you go out.

Winter

  • Cut holes in the ice with the axe. Jig for lake trout and burbot, and set lines under the ice overnight. Burbot feed at night in winter.
  • Set the gill net under the ice between two holes, drawn through with a long pole.
  • Keep 15 to 20 snares running.
  • Fire the stove hard for an hour or two in the evening, then let it burn out and sleep on the warm bench.

Keeping food

  • Smoked and dried fish keeps for months if it stays dry.
  • Once the days stay below freezing, fish and hares keep frozen on their own.
  • Hang every store high between two trees, well away from the shelter. Bears feed hard until they sleep in October, and wolverines and foxes raid caches all winter.
  • Clean fish at the water, away from camp.

A normal day in winter

  1. First light: check the ice lines and the net.
  2. Morning: walk the snare line. Cut and split the day's dead wood and pick out the grubs.
  3. Midday: eat the main meal. Mend gear.
  4. Afternoon: short work only, and rest. Every hour of hard work costs food.
  5. Evening: fire the stove for an hour or two, cook, then let it burn out.

When to walk out

  • Set your limit before you go and keep to it. For example, walk out if you lose a fifth of your starting weight, or can no longer do a full day's work.
  • Walk out at once for dizziness, confusion, fainting, a heartbeat that races or stumbles at rest, or swelling in the legs.
  • Walk out for any break through the ice, deep cut or frostbite you cannot treat.

Safety

  • Ice: stay off anything under 10 cm of clear ice. Grey or snow-covered ice is weaker. Carry a pole across your body when you walk out, and keep off the ice near inflows and outflows, where currents keep it thin.
  • Cold water: if you go through, turn back the way you came, kick flat onto the ice, and roll away. Get to the fire and into dry clothes at once.
  • Fish: cook fish from this lake, because raw fish can carry tapeworm.
  • Bears: keep food and fish waste away from the shelter until the bears den.
  • The stove: keep the vents open whenever it burns. If you get a headache, dizziness or nausea, get outside.
