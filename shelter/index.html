One-person shelter between three trees

A lean-to with log walls and a tarp roof under moss and dirt, framed on three living trees and one post, with the rocket mass stove inside. The stove's bench is the bed. Built with an axe, a saw, a knife and a tarp.

Site

  • Three healthy trees standing roughly in a right angle, about 3.2 m and 2.3 m apart, centre to centre. The front of the shelter faces south between two of them.
  • Trees with high crowns and no dead limbs over the shelter. Branches must be at least 2 m above the chimney top.
  • Level ground about 25 m back from the lake and well above its high-water line, with open water close for fishing and young spruce and willow nearby for hares.
  • The shore gives sand, stones and water for mixing. Dig clay from the banks of a stream running into the lake. Keep stones from the water away from the fire. Use them for the drain and the paths.

The stove in this shelter

The stove stands by the post, in the corner farthest from the trees. The bench runs along the back wall and turns along the west wall. The flue leaves through the west wall to a chimney between the two west trees, clear of the roof. Open the stove simulator

Building it in stages

Stage one, the first week. Build the base, the core with its cooktop, and the chimney. Lay the channel along the bench route as a trench lined with stones, covered with flat stones and sealed with clay. The stove cooks and warms the shelter while it burns, but stores little heat, so sleep on a bed of boughs.

Stage two, weeks two to four. Go on only if the clay is good and the weather is dry. Pack cob and dense stone over the duct one section at a time, until there is 20 cm over the channel. Leave the lift-out stones over the two bends. The bench then holds the heat through the night.

Cooking and water

  • The cooktop is the flat stone on top of the dome. Flame from the riser strikes its underside, so it is hottest over the riser and cooler toward the edges. Boil or fry in the middle and slide a pot to the edge to simmer.
  • The counter is a split log laid flat side up on stakes driven into the floor, 80 cm high. One leg runs along the east wall for food prep. The other runs along the front wall and holds the water basin.
  • The basin is a length of log hollowed by laying hot coals on it and scraping out the char, over and over. Fill it from the lake and keep a pot of water at the edge of the cooktop for hot water.
  • Throw used water out well away from the lake, not into the drain.

Camp for fishing and snares

  • Smoke rack: a frame of poles 1.5 m high above the shore, with a slow fire of green wood under it and a bark roof over the fish. The stove makes no smoke, so fish is smoked here.
  • Cleaning rock: a flat rock at the water, well along the shore from camp, so blood and guts wash away and draw no bears to the shelter.
  • Net poles: a crossbar on two poles at the shore for drying and mending the net.
  • Food cache: a pole lashed between two trees, with food hung from its middle, out of reach from the ground and from both trunks.
  • Winter cache: once everything stays frozen, fish and hares keep under a pile of stones on a log platform closer to camp.
  • Snare line: a marked trail through young spruce and willow on the hare runs, with a stump near the shelter for skinning. Hides make mitten and hat liners.
  • Ice gear: a long pole to carry across the ice and thread the net under it, a slotted wooden ladle for slush, and a gaff hook, all carved and kept by the door.
  • Inside: pegs on the wall for the net, lines and snare wire, a drying line high under the rafters away from the cooktop for mittens and socks, and a flat stone by the door for boots.
  • Paths: cleared paths to the shore, the snare line and the cache. Pack them when the snow comes.

Cleaning out

  • Every few days, rake the ash out of the burn tunnel through the feed tube.
  • Every month or two, lift the plug at the bottom of the dome and rake out the fine ash that settles there.
  • Once a year, lift the stones over the two bends in the bench and the plug at the foot of the chimney, and rake out the ash and soot. Check the clay lining in the chimney log at the same time.
  • If the stove starts to smoke back or pull weakly, clean everything before you change anything else.

Build order

  1. Clear to mineral soil. Cut a shallow ditch along the uphill side, behind the back wall, to turn rainwater around the shelter.
  2. Set the post at the fourth corner, one more halfway along the back, and one each side of the door. Lash a beam from the corner post to the north-west tree at 2.0 m, and a plate between the two south trees at 1.2 m. Pad the bark under each lashing with moss or bark and wrap it loosely, so the tree can sway and grow. Loosen the lashings every year.
  3. Lay rafters every 40 cm from beam to plate. Stretch the tarp over them and tie it down at the edges. Cover it with 10 cm of moss and dirt to hold it in the wind and shield it from sparks and sun. Stop the tarp 30 cm short of the west edge, by the chimney, and cover that strip with bark and dirt. Where the roof passes a trunk, cut the tarp around it with a hand's width to spare and lay bark over the gap.
  4. Under the roof, in the post corner, dig out 20 cm for the stove base. Lay 10 cm of dry stone, then 10 cm of light clay, so the top is level with the floor. Run a gravel drain 30 cm deep from the stone layer out under the east wall and downhill. Build the stove on the base as in the build guide, to the sizes in the table above. Lay the channel as a stone-lined duct under flat stones, and pack the bench over it later, as the stages above set out. Build the chimney in stone and cob to 1 m, then stand a hollow log lined thick with clay on top, up to 2.35 m, and lash it to a post driven in beside it. The channel climbs 10 cm from the stove into the bench. Leave 10 cm of light clay between the back wall and the stove and bench.
  5. Leave the cleanouts as you build: a flat stone plug at floor level in the face of the dome beside the feed tube, a removable stone in the bench top over each bend in the channel, and a plug at the foot of the chimney outside.
  6. Lay one course of flat stones, 15 cm high, under each wall to keep the bottom log off the wet ground. At each end of each wall and each side of the door, drive a pair of stakes, one inside the wall line and one outside. Stack logs 15 cm thick between the stakes, hewing the top and bottom of each log flat with the axe so they sit tight. Stop the logs 5 cm short of each trunk. Lash each pair of stakes together at the top. You need about 85 m of log in all.
  7. Stuff every gap between the logs with moss, and the gap at each trunk too, so the tree can sway. Smear clay over the moss only on the logs within 50 cm of the stove, and set the flue in stone and clay where it passes under the west wall, with a flat stone over it to carry the logs.
  8. Leave a gap in the second log of the front wall as an air inlet, and a gap in the top log of the back wall as a vent. Hang a hide or blanket in the door.
  9. Drive four stakes for the counter and lay split log halves across them. Burn and scrape a log for the basin. Set a log round beside the stove as a step up to the cooktop.
  10. Dry everything, then fire the stove small at first as the guide says.

Safety

  • Keep the low air inlet and the high vent open whenever the stove burns. If you get a headache, dizziness or nausea, get outside.
  • Let the fire burn out before you sleep. The bench holds the heat through the night.
  • Keep bedding, clothes and the woodpile off the cooktop and away from the feed tube.
  • Lay a flat hearth stone in front of the feed tube to catch sparks and ash, and keep the floor around it clear.
  • Look up before you build and after every storm. Pull down any dead branch over the shelter.
  • Trees sway in the wind. After a storm, check the lashings and repack the moss where the logs meet the trunks.
  • Keep the tarp away from the chimney. A spark melts a hole straight through it.
  • Keep branches cut back at least 2 m above the chimney top. Sparks land in them otherwise.
  • Check the clay lining inside the chimney log each season. If it has cracked or the wood has charred, reline it.
