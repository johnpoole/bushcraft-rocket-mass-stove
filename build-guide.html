Rocket mass stove from bushcraft materials

This is a rocket stove joined to a mass of earth, clay and stone that soaks up the heat. It burns small sticks hot and clean. The hot exhaust runs through a thick clay-and-stone bench before it leaves, and the bench gives off the heat for hours. Everything below can be gathered or made on site; no metal is needed.

Section drawing

How it works

 feed tube ──► burn tunnel ──► heat riser (insulated chimney) ──► dome (hot exhaust drops)
                                                                       │
             chimney ◄── channel through the bench (heat goes into the mass) ◄──┘

The riser is insulated, so it gets very hot and pulls hard, like a turbo. That strong pull drives the exhaust through the long bench channel.

Stone warning: only use stones from dry ground. Stones from rivers, lakes or wet ground can hold water and explode when heated. Avoid shale and layered or porous stone near the fire. Dense granite-type rock is best.

Mixes

  • Cob (the bulk material): about 1 part clay to 2–3 parts sand, with a good handful of fibre. Tread it together on a tarp or on bark.
  • Refractory mix (the burn tunnel only, the hottest part): 1 part clay to 1–2 parts sand. No fibre, because it burns out and leaves the wall weak.
  • Light clay insulation (around the riser): clay thinned to a slurry, stirred into sawdust, dry grass or ash until everything is just coated. You want it light and full of air.

Test your clay first: make a few small patties, dry them, and put them in the fire. Adjust the sand until they come out without cracking.

Sizing

The feed tube, burn tunnel, riser and all the channels need the same cross-section. About 10 × 10 cm works for a camp or shelter stove.

PartSize
Feed tube (vertical, sticks drop in)10 × 10 cm, about 30 cm tall
Burn tunnel (horizontal)10 × 10 cm, 30–40 cm long
Heat riser10 × 10 cm inside, at least 2–3× the burn tunnel length (80–120 cm)
Gap from riser top to dome ceiling5–8 cm
Bench channel10 × 10 cm, up to 3–4 m run, with as few sharp bends as you can manage
Exit chimney10 × 10 cm, top at least 60 cm above the riser top

If you undersize any section, it will choke the whole system and smoke will come out of the feed tube.

How the dimensions are measured

They are all inside measurements: the open space the fire and gas move through. Wall thickness goes on the outside of these numbers, so the formers you pack clay around should be these sizes.

Chimney top vs riser topCompare the top lip of each, measured from the same ground level

Measuring the riser from the tunnel floor and the tunnel along its inside floor keeps the 2–3× ratio honest.

If a wall ends up bumpy, make sure the narrowest point is still at least 10 × 10 cm. One pinch point chokes the draft as badly as undersizing the whole run. Cob also shrinks a little as it dries, so it's fine to make the formers about 1 cm oversize.

Build steps

1. Site and foundation

  • Choose level, well-drained ground, away from overhanging branches and flammable shelter walls.
  • Scrape off topsoil and leaf litter down to mineral soil.
  • Lay a layer of dry stones about 10 cm deep across the whole footprint of the core and bench. It keeps ground moisture out and stops heat soaking into the earth.

2. Burn tunnel and feed tube (the J-shape)

  • Lay a flat stone as the floor of the burn tunnel.
  • Make a former for the tunnel: a tight bundle of green sticks bound with bark, sized to 10 × 10 cm and long enough to run from the feed tube to the base of the riser.
  • Pack refractory mix around it, 5–8 cm thick.
  • Stand a second former upright at one end for the feed tube and pack refractory mix around it too.
  • Cap the tunnel with flat stones, or with thick refractory mix over the former.

3. Heat riser

  • Stand a tall former (a rolled bark tube or a stick bundle) at the far end of the tunnel.
  • Build a thin refractory inner wall around it, 2–3 cm thick.
  • Around that, pack light clay insulation 8–10 cm thick.
  • Finish with an outer shell of cob.
  • Build it up in layers, letting each firm up a little before adding the next, so it doesn't slump.

4. Dome (the bell)

  • Build a cob enclosure around the riser, leaving a gap of about 5 cm between the riser and the dome wall.
  • Leave the 5–8 cm gap above the riser top, and close the top with a large flat stone. That stone becomes your cooktop.
  • Where the dome meets the bench, open the outlet at the bottom of the dome. Hot gas rises up the riser, spreads under the cooktop, and falls down the gap to that outlet.

5. Bench and channel

  • Lay a former for the channel from the dome outlet: stick bundles, or a stone-lined trench with slabs over the top.
  • Run it along the length of the bench, with at least a slight rise toward the chimney end.
  • Bury it in cob and stones so there is 15–25 cm of mass above the channel. Dense stone packed in cob stores the most heat.
  • Build a cleanout: a removable stone plug at the dome base and at every bend, so you can rake out ash.

6. Exit chimney

  • At the far end of the bench, build a vertical flue from stacked stone and cob, or a hollow log lined thickly with clay (only at the cold end, where exhaust temperatures are low).
  • Its top must be higher than the riser top, and it must be clear of the shelter roof.

7. Seal and plaster

  • Smooth a clay-and-sand plaster over everything to seal cracks. Gas leaks mean smoke and CO.
  • Fill any cracks with slip.

Drying and first firing

  • Air-dry for as long as you can: several days, or longer in wet weather.
  • Small fires first. Burn a few handfuls of twigs just inside the feed tube for 1–2 days. This slowly drives out water and burns the formers away.
  • Rake out the ash and charred formers through the feed tube and the cleanouts.
  • Increase the fire size gradually over several days. Steam and hairline cracks are normal; seal the cracks with slip as they appear.
  • It's working when the fire roars sideways and is pulled down the feed tube, with little or no smoke from the chimney.

Running it

  • Fuel: dry, finger-to-wrist-thick sticks, standing upright in the feed tube. Push them down as they burn. The fire stays at the bottom and burns sideways.
  • Starting it: warm the riser first with a small hot tinder fire at its base. If smoke rolls back out of the feed tube, the system is cold or blocked.
  • Cooking: use the stone cap on the dome.
  • Heat: fire it hard for 1–3 hours. The bench gives off heat for 6–12+ hours afterwards.

Safety

  • Carbon monoxide: a cracked or badly drafting stove inside an enclosed shelter can kill. Keep ventilation and seal every crack. If you get a headache, dizziness or nausea, get outside now.
  • Exploding stones: only use dry-land stones near the heat.
  • Fire spread: keep a clear zone of about 1 m, bare to mineral soil, and have water or earth ready. Keep flammable shelter materials away from the riser, dome and chimney.
  • Never leave it burning unattended, and never put a lid on the feed tube while it's burning.
  • Follow local fire rules and leave no trace when you break the camp down.
Quick test before you commit: build a small bare-bones version first, with just a feed tube, burn tunnel and a short insulated riser. It will show you whether your clay and your proportions draft properly before you spend a day building the bench.
