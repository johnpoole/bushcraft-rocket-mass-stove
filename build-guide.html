Rocket mass stove from bushcraft materials

This is a rocket stove joined to a mass of earth, clay and stone that soaks up the heat. It burns small sticks hot and clean. The hot exhaust runs through a thick clay-and-stone bench before it leaves, and the bench gives off the heat for hours. Everything below can be gathered or made on site; no metal is needed.

This guide builds the stove for the one-person shelter between three trees, in two stages: a working stove in the first week, and the heat-storing bench once the clay and weather allow. It ends with the fish weir for the camp.

How it works

 feed tube ──► burn tunnel ──► heat riser (insulated chimney) ──► dome (hot exhaust drops)
                                                                       │
             chimney ◄── channel through the bench (heat goes into the mass) ◄──┘

The riser is insulated, so it gets very hot and pulls hard, like a turbo. That strong pull drives the exhaust through the long bench channel.

Stone warning: only use stones from dry ground. Stones from rivers, lakes or wet ground can hold water and explode when heated. Avoid shale and layered or porous stone near the fire. Dense granite-type rock is best.

Mixes

  • Cob (the bulk material): about 1 part clay to 2–3 parts sand, with a good handful of fibre. Tread it together on a tarp or on bark.
  • Refractory mix (the burn tunnel only, the hottest part): 1 part clay to 1–2 parts sand. No fibre, because it burns out and leaves the wall weak.
  • Light clay insulation (around the riser): clay thinned to a slurry, stirred into sawdust, dry grass or ash until everything is just coated. You want it light and full of air.

Test your clay first: make a few small patties, dry them, and put them in the fire. Adjust the sand until they come out without cracking.

Measuring the riser from the tunnel floor and the tunnel along its inside floor keeps the riser-to-tunnel ratio honest.

If a wall ends up bumpy, make sure the narrowest point is still at least 11 × 11 cm. One pinch point chokes the draft as badly as undersizing the whole run. Cob also shrinks a little as it dries, so it's fine to make the formers about 1 cm oversize.

Stage one: a working stove in the first week

Stage one takes about 0.7 m³ of clay, sand and stone, about 1.2 tonnes, most of it in the chimney and core. When it is dry the stove cooks and warms the shelter while it burns, but stores little heat. Sleep on a bed of boughs until stage two.

1. Site and base

  • Find the clay before you commit to the site. The east arm is mostly bare granite, and the whole stove depends on workable clay within easy carrying distance. Dig test holes in creek banks and low ground in the first days. No clay, no stove: pick another site or plan on an open fire.
  • Build under the shelter roof, in the corner farthest from the trees, so rain stays off the clay while it dries.
  • Scrape off topsoil and leaf litter down to mineral soil.
  • Under the stove, dig out 20 cm. Lay 10 cm of dry stone, then 10 cm of light clay, so the top is level with the floor. The stone drains and the light clay keeps the heat out of the ground.
  • Run a gravel drain 30 cm deep from the stone layer out under the wall and downhill.
  • Lay 10 cm of dry stone along the whole bench route.

2. Burn tunnel and feed tube (the J-shape)

  • Lay a flat stone on the base as the floor of the burn tunnel. It sits level with the shelter floor. If you have time, lay pit-fired clay bricks on top of it instead: the tunnel floor wears fastest, and fired clay lasts far longer than raw.
  • Make a former for the tunnel: a tight bundle of dry grass or withies bound with bark, 11 × 11 cm and 40 cm long, from the feed tube to the base of the riser. Pull it out once the walls have firmed up. Green wood left inside wet clay chars slowly and leaves debris in the flue.
  • Pack refractory mix around it, 5–8 cm thick.
  • Stand a second former upright at one end for the feed tube, 30 cm tall, and pack refractory mix around it too.
  • Cap the tunnel with flat stones, or with thick refractory mix over the former.
  • Lay a flat hearth stone on the floor in front of the feed tube.

3. Heat riser

  • Weave a basket tube of dry withies 110 cm tall and 11 × 11 cm inside, and stand it at the far end of the tunnel as the riser former. It holds its shape while you build and burns out cleanly on the first fires.
  • Build a thin refractory inner wall around it, 3 cm thick.
  • Around that, pack 8 cm of light clay insulation.
  • Build it up in layers, letting each firm up a little before adding the next, so it doesn't slump.

4. Dome and cooktop

  • Build a cob dome around the riser, 8 cm thick, with a 5 cm gap between the riser and the dome wall.
  • Leave a 6 cm gap above the riser top, and close the top with a large flat stone. That stone is the cooktop. It sits about 1.2 m up, so set a log round 25 cm high beside the stove as a step.
  • Open the outlet at the bottom of the dome on the bench side. Hot gas rises up the riser, spreads under the cooktop, and falls down the gap to that outlet.
  • Leave a cleanout at floor level in the face of the dome beside the feed tube: a gap closed with a flat stone plug, sealed with clay.

5. Duct

  • From the dome outlet, dig and build a trench along the bench route, lined with stones to a 11 × 11 cm channel. It climbs 10 cm from the dome to the bench base, then runs level or rising slightly toward the chimney.
  • Cover it with flat stones and seal the joints with clay. Over each of the two bends, lay a stone you can lift out to rake the ash.

6. Chimney

  • At the end of the duct, outside the wall, build the chimney in stone and cob all the way to 2.35 m above the floor, 40 cm square outside. Lay flat stones through the cob every few courses to tie it together.
  • Keep wood out of the flue entirely. In stage one there is no bench mass to take the heat out of the exhaust, and while the stove starts up the gas reaching the chimney is far hotter than the 100°C it settles to once the bench is built. A burning chimney would take the shelter with it.
  • Its top must be at least 60 cm above the roof beside it, and branches must be at least 2 m above the top.
  • Leave a cleanout plug at the foot of the chimney.

7. Seal

  • Smooth a clay-and-sand plaster over the dome, the duct joints and the chimney base to seal cracks. Gas leaks mean smoke and CO.
  • Fill any cracks with slip.

Stage two: the bench, weeks two to four

Go on only if the clay is good. Stage two adds about 0.8 m³ of clay and stone, close to one and a half tonnes.

The problem is water, not the bench. Built as plain cob, the bench holds about 250 litres of water. While the stove burns, about 3 kW goes into the bench. Three hours of firing a day can drive off at most about 15 litres, and in practice more like 5–8, so plain cob would take five to seven weeks to dry. Put less water in, and it dries in one to three weeks.

  • Build it mostly of stone. Pack dry-land stones tight over the duct and use clay only as mortar between them, about six parts stone to four of clay. That cuts the water to about 100 litres, and dense stone stores more heat than cob does.
  • Or make blocks ahead of time. From the first days, mould blocks of clay and sand about 20 × 10 × 8 cm and dry them wherever there is heat: on the smoke rack frame, stacked around the dome, and on the stage-one duct. Lay them in thin clay mortar. Only the mortar is left to dry, about 10–20 litres.
  • Or ram it. Tamp barely damp clay, sand and gravel in 10 cm layers between two logs held apart with stakes. Rammed earth takes about half the water of cob.
  • Build in thin lifts over the hot duct. Lay 5–8 cm at a time. Drying time grows with the square of the thickness, so a thin lift dries in a day or two where a full 20 cm slab takes weeks. The duct dries it from underneath.
  • Fire little and often. Two or three short fires a day dry it faster than one long one, and heating wet clay slowly keeps it from cracking.
  • Ventilate while it dries. Open the door and the vent during and after each firing, or the steam condenses on the tarp and soaks into your sleeping bag.
  • Keep it above freezing. Frost outside is not the limit: a shelter fired every evening, with a warm duct under the bench, keeps the wet sections above freezing. Cover them with dry grass at night. Stop adding wet material only if the shelter itself freezes overnight, because wet clay that freezes crumbles.
  • Check before you seal. Lay a piece of bark on the bench overnight. If it is damp underneath in the morning, the bench is still wet. Plaster the top last.
  • Build it to a sleeping bench along the back wall and a seat along the side, 45 cm high, with 20 cm over the channel.
  • Keep the lift-out stones over the two bends at the bench surface, so you can still reach them.
  • Keep 10 cm of light clay between the bench and the wall.

Until the bench is done, sleep warm another way: heat a few dry-land stones the size of a loaf on the cooktop while the stove burns, wrap them in a spare layer, and put them in the sleeping bag at your feet and belly. Lay the bough bed on a thick layer of dry grass over the stage-one duct, which still gives off some heat after a firing.

Drying and first firing

  • Air-dry for as long as you can: several days, or longer in wet weather.
  • Small fires first. Burn a few handfuls of twigs just inside the feed tube for 1–2 days. This slowly drives out water and burns out the woven riser former.
  • Rake out the ash and any charred former through the feed tube and the cleanouts.
  • Increase the fire size gradually over several days. Steam and hairline cracks are normal; seal the cracks with slip as they appear.
  • It's working when the fire roars sideways and is pulled down the feed tube, with little or no smoke from the chimney.

Running it

  • Fuel: dry, finger-to-wrist-thick sticks, standing upright in the feed tube. Push them down as they burn. The fire stays at the bottom and burns sideways.
  • Starting it: warm the riser first with a small hot tinder fire at its base. If smoke rolls back out of the feed tube, the system is cold or blocked.
  • Cooking: stand on the step and use the stone on top of the dome. It is hottest over the riser. Slide a pot toward the edge to simmer.
  • Heat: fire it hard for 1–3 hours. After stage two the bench gives off heat for about 16 hours afterwards. Let the fire burn out before you sleep.

Cleaning out

  • Every few days, rake the ash out of the burn tunnel through the feed tube.
  • Every month or two, lift the plug at the bottom of the dome and rake out the fine ash that settles there.
  • Once a year, lift the stones over the two bends and the plug at the foot of the chimney, and rake out the ash and soot. Check the chimney for cracks at the same time and seal them with clay.
  • If the stove starts to smoke back or pull weakly, clean everything before you change anything else.

Fish weir

A weir is a fence of stakes across a creek that leads fish into a pen. Once it is built it fishes day and night, and all you do is empty the pen. Build it in the first two weeks, before the stove, where a creek runs into the lake. Lake whitefish and cisco move into the shallows and creek mouths to spawn from about September into November.

Materials

  • 60 to 80 straight saplings or split sticks, 3–5 cm thick and about 1.2 m long, sharpened at one end.
  • Willow or spruce branches to weave between them.
  • Stones from the creek, to close the gaps along the bottom.

Build

  • Choose a spot a few metres above the creek mouth where the water is knee-deep or less and the bed is gravel you can drive stakes into.
  • From each bank, drive a line of stakes into the bed with a rock, no more than 3 cm apart. Angle the two lines upstream so they meet in a V pointing upstream, and leave a gap of 10 cm at the point.
  • Upstream of the gap, drive a ring of stakes 1.6 m across as the pen. The gap is its only way in.
  • Weave branches between the stakes, and pile stones along the bottom of the fence and the pen so fish can't slip under.
  • Keep the stakes at least 30 cm above the water so fish can't jump over.

Fish swimming up the creek follow the fence to its point, pass through the gap, and circle inside the pen without finding the way out.

Using it

  • Check the pen morning and evening. Lift the fish out by hand or with the gaff.
  • Clear leaves and drift off the fence after rain, and repair it after high water.
  • Set the 12 m gill net straight out from the creek mouth into the lake, where fish gather and move along the shore toward the creek.
  • Clean fish on a rock along the shore, well away from the weir, so guts don't foul it.
  • While the creek still flows under its ice, keep a hole open over the pen. Pull the stakes before the ice freezes solid, or it will crush them.

Safety

  • Carbon monoxide: a cracked or badly drafting stove inside an enclosed shelter can kill. Keep ventilation and seal every crack. If you get a headache, dizziness or nausea, get outside now.
  • Exploding stones: only use dry-land stones near the heat.
  • Fire spread: keep a clear zone of about 1 m, bare to mineral soil, and have water or earth ready. Keep 10 cm of light clay between the stove and bench and any log wall, and smear clay over the logs near the stove. Keep a tarp roof well away from the chimney, since a spark melts through it.
  • Never leave it burning unattended, and never put a lid on the feed tube while it's burning.
  • Follow local fire rules and leave no trace when you break the camp down.
Quick test before you commit: build a small bare-bones version first, with just a feed tube, burn tunnel and a short insulated riser, outside and away from the shelter. It will show you whether your clay and your proportions draft properly before you spend a week on stage one.
